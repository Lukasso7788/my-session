## The Pomodoro Technique, with other people

The Pomodoro Technique is one of the best-known focus methods: work for a fixed interval, usually 25 minutes, take a short break, and repeat, with a longer break after several rounds. It was developed by Francesco Cirillo, who named it after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer.

A **group Pomodoro session** keeps the same rhythm but shares it. Everyone in the room follows one timer, starts focusing together, and takes breaks together, while each person works on their own task. It is sometimes called Pomodoro coworking. This article explains what changes when the timer is shared, how to pick an interval, and how to run a good round.

## What changes when the timer is shared

A solo timer is easy to ignore. You can pause it, restart it, or let the break stretch to twenty minutes without anyone noticing. A shared timer is harder to bend, because the room keeps moving whether or not you do.

That creates three useful effects:

- **A fixed start.** You do not decide when to begin; the next focus block decides for you. This removes one of the hardest choices of the day.
- **Breaks that end.** When the room returns to focus, you return too. Breaks stay restful instead of turning into a lost afternoon.
- **Quiet presence.** Seeing others work through the same interval is a form of [body doubling](/guides/what-is-body-doubling). You are not alone with the timer.

The trade-off is flexibility. If you are deep in a problem when the break starts, you may want to keep going. That is fine: a shared timer is a structure, not an order. Finish your sentence, then step away.

## Choosing an interval

The classic 25/5 rhythm is a good default, but it is not the only option. Choose based on how hard it is to start and how much context the task needs.

| Rhythm | Good for |
| --- | --- |
| 15 focus / 3 break | Very low energy, a task you have avoided for days |
| 25 / 5 | Starting difficult work, email, admin, revision |
| 30 / 10 | A balanced block with a proper reset |
| 50 / 10 | Writing, coding, or study that needs momentum |
| 75 / 15 | Deep work with materials already prepared |

Always-open rooms also run repeating structures such as 30/10, 75/15, and a "Ladder" that grows from 10 to 25 minutes with short check-ins between blocks. ## How to run a good round

1. **Before the timer:** write one checkable outcome for this round, such as "outline sections one and two." Our guide to [writing a focus session intention](/blog/how-to-write-a-focus-session-intention) has more examples.
2. **During focus:** work only on that outcome. If something else comes to mind, note it on a list and return to the task.
3. **At the break:** stand up and look away from the screen. Do not start a new task or open a feed you will struggle to close.
4. **Between rounds:** decide whether to continue the same outcome or move to the next one. A quick written note keeps the next round from starting with "where was I?"

## Group Pomodoro vs a solo timer app

A solo timer is the right tool when you already work well alone and only need a reminder to take breaks. A shared room adds social structure for days when starting, returning, or stopping on time is the problem.

Several products offer shared timers. As of October 2026, FocusPair advertises free video coworking with a built-in Pomodoro timer and no sign-up, and CSW Study Stream offers student rooms with a synced timer. MySession combines the shared timeline with live rooms, personal intentions, a task list, and optional music, in both scheduled sessions and always-open rooms. Compare current features before you commit, and choose the setup you will actually return to.

## Start with one round

You do not need to plan a full day of Pomodoros. Pick one task, choose a 25-minute block, and [join a room](/sessions) where the next focus stage is about to begin. If one round goes well, stay for the next. If it does not, change the interval, not your whole system. Read more about [virtual coworking rooms](/virtual-coworking) to see how the room stages work.
