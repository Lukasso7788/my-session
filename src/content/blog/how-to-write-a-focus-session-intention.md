## Why the intention matters more than the timer

Most focus sessions begin with a short moment to say what you plan to do. It is tempting to rush it with "work on the project" and get to the timer. But the intention is the part that decides whether the next 25 or 50 minutes go anywhere. A timer protects time; an intention gives that time a direction.

A good focus session intention is one sentence that names a finish line you can check. This guide shows how to write one quickly, with examples for common kinds of work.

## The three-part test

Before the session starts, check your intention against three questions:

1. **Can I tell when it is done?** "Write the introduction" has an end. "Work on the report" does not.
2. **Does it fit this session?** If the task would take three hours, pick the part that fits the block you chose.
3. **Do I know the first action?** If you cannot picture the first click or the first sentence, the intention is still too vague.

If the answer to any question is no, make the intention smaller. A small finished outcome is worth more than a large unfinished one, both for the work and for your confidence in the next session.

## Turn vague tasks into checkable ones

| Vague | Specific and checkable |
| --- | --- |
| Work on my thesis | Draft 300 words of the methods section |
| Do email | Reply to the four flagged emails, archive the rest |
| Study biology | Complete practice questions 1-15 and mark the ones I miss |
| Clean the kitchen | Empty the dishwasher and clear the counter |
| Fix the bug | Reproduce the login error and write down the steps |
| Plan next week | List the three deadlines and block time for each |

Notice the pattern: a verb, an object, and a boundary such as a number, a section, or a clear end state.

## When you do not know the outcome yet

Some work is exploratory. You may not know what "done" looks like for research, design, or a problem you have not solved before. In that case, set an intention around the process instead of the result:

- "Spend this block listing possible causes, without fixing anything yet."
- "Try three layout options and keep the best one."
- "Read the first two sources and write one paragraph of notes on each."

These are still checkable. At the end you can say whether you listed the causes, tried the layouts, or wrote the notes.

## Keep it private when you need to

In a shared room, you do not have to reveal who your client is, what your company is working on, or anything personal. Name the type of work and the boundary: "Finish the first draft of a proposal" or "Review a contract, sections one to three." The intention helps you, not the audience. The guide to [virtual coworking etiquette](/blog/virtual-coworking-etiquette) has more on keeping shared rooms comfortable for everyone.

## Use the check-in to write the next intention

The end of a session is the easiest time to plan the next one, because you know exactly where you stopped. At the check-in, answer two questions:

- **What changed?** "Methods section has 300 words; the sampling paragraph still needs a citation."
- **What is the first action next time?** "Find the citation for the sampling paragraph."

That second answer becomes your next intention. You start the following session with a clear step instead of a fresh round of deciding. This is the same habit described in the [remote-work accountability routine](/blog/remote-work-accountability-without-meetings).
