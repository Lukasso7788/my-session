## The part of the session people are tempted to skip

Most people join a body doubling session for the quiet part: a shared timer, other people working, and a block of time to get something done. The short moments when everyone unmutes and talks can feel like an interruption. It is tempting to stay muted, let others speak, and get back to work.

That would be a mistake. The verbal check-in is small, but it is the part of the session that turns "being in a room with people" into accountability. This guide explains what a check-in is, why checking in after every focus block matters so much, and how to do it well.

## What a verbal check-in is

A verbal check-in is the practice of reporting your progress out loud after each focus block. When the timer ends, you unmute and tell the room, the other coworkers and the host, what you got done, how it went, and what you will do next. You are not just working next to other people; you are sharing your progress with them.

In a body doubling session, everyone works on their own task while sharing a room and a timer. The check-in is the moment that connects those separate tasks: for twenty seconds, your work becomes something other people hear about.

In MySession sessions with verbal check-ins, that happens at three points:

1. **At the start.** Each person names one goal for the first focus block.
2. **After each focus block.** A quick round by name: what you got done, what is next, and anything in your way. Then everyone mutes and takes a real break.
3. **At the end.** A short wrap-up of what you finished and what you will do next time.

Focus blocks themselves stay quiet: microphones off, cameras on. The talking happens only in the short windows between them.

If body doubling is new to you, [what body doubling is](/guides/what-is-body-doubling) explains the basics.

## Why verbal check-ins are so important

**It pulls you into the structure.** A session has a rhythm: focus, check in, break, focus again. Without a check-in, it is easy to sit at the edge of that rhythm, half-working with the timer running in a background tab. A check-in after every block asks you to take part. You show up at each round, you report, you set the next goal, and the session's structure becomes your structure.

**It raises your accountability.** Knowing that in 25 or 50 minutes you will tell real people what you did changes how you spend those minutes. Accountability is the reason body doubling works, and the check-in is where it becomes concrete: a host and other coworkers who heard your goal will hear how it went.

**Saying a goal out loud makes it real.** A goal you only think about is easy to quietly replace with something more comfortable. A goal you said to four people is a small public commitment. You do not need anyone to hold you to it; knowing you said it is usually enough.

**Every block gets its own deadline.** Without check-ins, a three-hour session can blur into one long stretch where drifting for twenty minutes feels harmless. When you know you will report back in 25 or 50 minutes, each block becomes a short loop with a clear finish line. That near deadline is much easier to work toward than the end of the day.

**Drift gets caught early.** If a block went badly, you find out at the check-in, not at 6 p.m. Saying "I got distracted, I am going to try a smaller piece" is often the reset that saves the rest of the session.

**Progress becomes visible.** Hearing yourself say "done" a few times in a row builds momentum. Hearing others report progress is a quiet nudge that work is happening around you, which is the core of body doubling.

**It makes the room feel human.** A few sentences between blocks are enough to turn a grid of strangers into people you recognize. That connection is part of why people come back for the next session.

**Speaking is stronger than typing.** A written note in chat is better than nothing, and it is a fine option when you cannot talk. But saying a goal in your own voice, to people who are listening, carries more weight than a line of text that scrolls away. It is harder to say "I will finish the draft" out loud and then spend the block on something else.

## Even stronger together: check-ins plus the rest of the room

A verbal check-in is powerful on its own. Combined with the other accountability features in a MySession room, it becomes one of the most effective productivity practices there is, because each one covers a different moment of the session:

- **Camera on** means people see you working during the quiet block.
- **Screen sharing** means they can see what you are working on, not just that you are at your desk.
- **A pinned Tasks panel** keeps your goal and the timer in front of you the whole block, so you know exactly what you will report.
- **Picture-in-Picture** keeps the room in view when you switch to another app.
- **The verbal check-in** closes the loop: you say what you planned, and after the block you say what you did.

Together they keep you on track from the first minute to the last. Being seen keeps you working, a visible goal keeps you focused on the right thing, and the check-in turns all of it into a commitment you renew after every block. ## How a session with verbal check-ins runs

Here is a typical 50/10 session with check-ins after every block:

| Stage | What happens | Voice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start | Quick hello, each person names a goal for block one | Talk |
| Focus, 50 min | Everyone works on their own task | Quiet, mics off |
| Check-in and break, 10 min | Round by name, about 20 seconds each, then a real break | Talk, then quiet |
| Focus, 50 min | Next block, with the goal you just named | Quiet, mics off |
| End | What you finished and what is next | Talk |

![A session timeline with two quiet 50-minute focus blocks, a spoken check-in between them, and three short check-in messages](/blog/editorial/verbal-check-in-rhythm.png)

The same rhythm works with shorter blocks. In a 25/5 Pomodoro session, the check-in is shorter and the round moves faster. Our guide to [group Pomodoro sessions](/blog/group-pomodoro-sessions) covers that format in more detail.

## What to say at a check-in

A good check-in takes about 20 seconds and answers three questions:

- **What did I get done?** One concrete result, not a description of the whole project.
- **What is next?** The goal for the next block, specific enough to check.
- **Anything in my way?** Optional. One sentence, if something is blocking you.

For example: "I finished the outline. Next block I will draft the introduction. No blockers." Or: "Honestly, not much, I kept checking email. Next block I will just write the first two paragraphs with email closed."

Both are good check-ins. The second one is arguably more useful, because saying it out loud is what turns a lost block into a reset. Nobody in the room is grading you.

If you struggle to phrase the next goal, our guide to [writing a focus session intention](/blog/how-to-write-a-focus-session-intention) has before-and-after examples.

## Check-in etiquette

**Keep it short.** Twenty to thirty seconds per person keeps the round moving and leaves time for an actual break.

**Share the outcome, not the private details.** "Finished the client report" is enough; you do not need to name the client or the project. More on that in our guide to [virtual coworking etiquette](/blog/virtual-coworking-etiquette).

**Listen, do not coach.** A check-in is not a discussion. Unless someone asks for help, a short "nice" or a reaction is plenty.

**Mute again when the round ends.** The break belongs to everyone, and the next focus block starts quiet.

**Do not skip your turn.** It is tempting to stay muted and let others talk, especially on a bad day. Those are exactly the days the check-in helps most.

## Verbal, start-and-end, or silent: which to choose

Not every session uses check-ins after every block. Hosts on MySession can run three styles:

| Style | Start | Between blocks | End | Good for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fully verbal | Talk | Talk | Talk | Regular groups, accountability buddies |
| Verbal start and end | Talk | Chat | Talk | Most sessions |
| Silent | Chat | Quiet | Chat | Deep work, first sessions, shy groups |

Talking is always optional. If you are not comfortable speaking, a written check-in in the room chat does the same job: you still name your goal and report back. Many people start with chat and switch to speaking once the room feels familiar.

Choose fully verbal sessions when you tend to drift between blocks, when you want the strongest accountability, or when you are working on something you have been avoiding. Choose a quieter style when the work needs long unbroken concentration or when talking would cost you more energy than it gives.

## Getting the most from your next verbal session

- **Write your first goal before you join.** Add it to the Tasks panel so you are not inventing it on the spot.
- **Keep your camera on.** Being seen during the quiet blocks and heard during the check-ins work together.
- **Have your next goal ready before the timer ends.** Then your check-in takes ten seconds.
- **Report honestly.** A short "it did not go well" plus a smaller next goal is a perfectly good check-in.

When you are ready, [find a session](/sessions), turn your camera on, pin your tasks, and take your turn at the first check-in.
